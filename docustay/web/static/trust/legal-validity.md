# Is an electronic signature legal?

In the United States, federal and state law generally treat an electronic signature as acceptable for many documents. Here is what the ESIGN Act and UETA look for in plain words, and what a Docustay signature is.

## What makes an electronic signature hold up.

1. **Intent**: A deliberate act to sign
2. **Consent**: Agreed to sign electronically
3. **Association**: Tied to the record
4. **Records**: Kept and reproducible

## Each element, and what is kept.

- **Intent**: The signer opens the document, fills the fields, ticks that they agree to sign electronically, draws or types a signature and presses Finish and sign. The act, its time, address and browser are stored.
- **Consent**: Before the first signature the signer ticks a statement that they agree to receive and sign the document electronically. Its wording and version are stored with the time, address and browser of the acceptance.
- **Statement**: It says the signer can read the whole document first, can keep or print a copy and can ask the sender for a paper copy. If they do not agree they can close the page and nothing is recorded.

- **Attribution**: The invitation goes to the signer's email as a single-use link, optionally with a code. Attribution rests on control of that address and the code.
- **Association**: The signature is placed on the document and the finished file is sealed with a digital signature over the whole file, including the certificate page. A SHA-256 fingerprint is printed on the certificate.
- **Keeping**: The sealed PDF and the audit trail are stored with the workspace. A signed document cannot be deleted one by one; only the owner deleting the whole workspace removes it.
- **Time**: Local time and UTC are printed, and a trusted RFC 3161 timestamp is added when the time service answers.

## The ESIGN Act and UETA, in plain words.

- **What it is**: A US federal law about electronic signatures and records
- **In short**: A signature or record is not denied effect only because it is electronic, for many kinds of document
- **Consumers**: A consumer must agree to electronic records after a disclosure

- **What it is**: A model law that most US states have adopted in some form
- **In short**: The same idea at state level: an electronic signature can satisfy a signature requirement when the people meant to sign
- **Differences**: States differ in the details

## What a Docustay signature is, and when you may need something else.

- **Kind**: A simple electronic signature with a strong evidence record and a tamper-evident seal
- **Check**: Anyone can check a signed copy on the verify page with no account

- **Qualified**: Docustay does not produce qualified electronic signatures
- **Identity**: It does not check identity documents; it rests on control of an email address and any code you share
- **Excluded documents**: Some kinds of document are excluded by law in many places, and Docustay does not decide which
- **The seal**: The sealing certificate is Docustay's own, so some PDF viewers show validity as unknown until told to trust it

## What the signer does, and what we keep.

The signer opens the document, fills the fields, ticks that they agree to sign electronically, draws or types a signature and presses Finish and sign. The act, its time, address and browser are stored.

**Consent.** A disclosure is shown first; its text and version are frozen into the record.

## A certificate page ends every signed copy.

Each person, the time and address of each act, the document's SHA-256 fingerprint and the audit trail.

## Questions

**Is it valid everywhere?**

We do not say that. Rules differ by place and by document. See the figures above.

**Can I check a signed PDF?**

Yes. Drop it on the verify page. It needs no account.

**Is this legal advice?**

No. It is general information about how signing works. It does not promise any legal outcome for any document. If a document matters, ask someone qualified where you are.
